# Russia: Simulation Report

Team 2: Steve (Executive and Technology Strategy Czar), Gloria (Intelligence and Immigration Czar), Tiger (Education Czar)

## 1. How the game unfolded

Russia entered the game in a weak position. It had the smallest budget of any country, its universities were weaker than its rivals', and its pool of skilled talent shrank every round. Its main advantage was experience: Russia began with more accumulated know-how than any other country, a legacy of Soviet science, and its closed borders meant that no rival could steal its research. In the terms of the course's "twin pillars" framework, Russia was strong in accumulated knowledge but weak in both people and institutions. Our brief therefore proposed "save, then strike": build up the budget, let other countries pay for public research, and later turn our wealth into high-value research. The game unfolded in three stages.

**Stage one: building.** In the early rounds we saved most of our budget, because unspent money grew our economy and enlarged every later budget, and we bought only the cheapest, safest research. Rather than funding basic research ourselves, we relied on the public research of the US and China, which also unlocked advanced research for us. This is the public goods problem from the course: basic knowledge is costly and uncertain to produce, and once it exists others can use it, so countries are tempted to let someone else pay. We also tested a different idea, discussed in Section 3.

**Stage two: setbacks.** By the middle of the game our safe research had reached its limit, and the advanced research levels had opened, largely because of our rivals' spending. We closed our borders again and began investing in advanced research. Our advanced research failed several rounds in a row, and our only gains came from espionage. We kept investing anyway; as the story of extreme ultraviolet lithography shows, turning research into real capability often requires repeated failure and sustained funding.

**Stage three: breakthrough.** With a much larger budget, we put most of our resources into building know-how, because the rules limited how much advanced research we could buy each round and know-how made each attempt far more likely to succeed. The approach finally worked. Our medium-level research began paying its full reward, and in one round our radical research produced a major breakthrough, the most valuable single result in the game. In the final rounds we added further medium-level gains and copied some of the US's research through espionage.

## 2. Our beliefs about Russia's progress

Because every country's research results were secret, we judged our position from indirect signals: each country's wealth, talent, border policy, and public research, along with what our spies revealed. Early in the game we believed Russia was behind in research but ahead in strategy. The US was attracting talent far faster than anyone else, but our spies showed that the leading countries had not yet built a large research lead. We also noticed that the US and China were saving too, which told us our budget advantage would be temporary.

The middle of the game was our low point. China and France were reporting advanced research results while ours kept failing, and China had become the wealthiest country. As a team we debated whether our strategy was flawed or simply unlucky. We concluded that our approach was sound and that the failures were mostly chance, so we kept investing in know-how rather than changing course.

After our radical breakthrough, our assessment changed. Because radical results count far more than any other kind, we believed Russia had likely taken the lead. Our main uncertainty was whether the US or China had achieved a similar breakthrough that we could not see. This echoed the course's discussion of measuring technology competition: different indicators can give different answers about who is "ahead," and the signals we could see did not reveal the secret results that decided the outcome.

## 3. How and why our strategy evolved

Our Round 1 strategy was to keep our borders closed for the whole game, skip education because Russia's talent drains away, save heavily, and free-ride on other countries' basic research. Three developments reshaped it.

**Testing whether Russia could reverse its brain drain.** Before the game, Professor Canfil asked whether Russia might do the opposite of our plan: open its borders and invest in universities to attract talent, especially if other countries closed their doors out of fear of espionage. The course identifies two ways to build human capital, educating people at home or attracting talent from abroad, and Zweig's account of how China reversed its brain drain suggested the second could work. We tested the idea in the early rounds by investing in universities and opening our borders while we had little research worth stealing. The test showed that Russia could not yet compete for talent. Our talent continued to shrink, and even when the US briefly closed its borders, Russia was not attractive enough to capture the talent it released. The lecture on talent flow explains why: strong universities attract talent, talent produces discoveries, and discoveries bring prestige and resources, a cycle that favors countries already at the top. China's success took years of funding and supportive institutions, and Kreps describes universities as long-term national infrastructure. Russia's weak institutions made this path unrealistic in the time available, so we closed our borders again to protect our growing research.

**Balancing openness and security.** Our border policy also reflected the research security debate from the guest lecture. U.S. policy has long treated open research as a strength, while Walker-Munro emphasizes managing security risks without shutting down valuable collaboration, and the story of Qian Xuesen shows how excluding talent out of suspicion can help a rival. Our own border policy moved through three steps. We began closed, as our brief recommended. Early in the game we opened our borders, hoping to attract talent and make our investment in universities pay off. When that did not work, we closed again and stayed closed for the rest of the game, because by then we had valuable results to protect and openness was bringing us little in return. Other countries also switched back and forth, often right after catching spies, and paid both costs: they lost security while open and lost talent while closed. Our experience suggests that openness pays off mainly for countries that can actually attract talent; for weaker countries, the security costs arrive before the benefits.

**Making know-how our main tool.** The game turned out to be shorter than we had planned, and since the final round was never announced, anything saved at the end would be lost. We therefore shifted from saving to spending earlier, choosing the option that worked however many rounds remained: investing in know-how immediately. This also matched Russia's real strength. Mokyr distinguishes between knowing why something works and knowing how to do it, and our advantage lay in the second kind of knowledge. Gilli and Gilli argue that this hands-on, tacit knowledge cannot simply be copied or stolen, and Cheung shows that acquired technology is useful only to a country that can absorb it. In the game, our spies could copy rivals' results, but our own know-how could not be taken. This reflects the course's appropriation problem: because copying is cheaper than inventing, rivals are tempted to take others' results instead of producing their own. Throughout the game we made decisions using the rule in our brief: a majority of the three of us decided, and the executive broke any ties.

## 4. Outcome and what we would do differently

Based on the information available at the end of the game, we believe Russia finished at or near the top. Our strategy worked for three reasons. First, saving early turned the smallest budget in the game into one of the largest, which paid for the know-how that made our research succeed. Second, free-riding on other countries' public research saved us a great deal, since their spending unlocked advanced research for us. Third, closing our borders again once we had valuable research protected our most important results from theft. Rivals also made costly choices: spies were caught repeatedly, the US and China kept switching their border policies, and France never built enough talent or wealth to reach the most valuable research.

Looking back, we would change four things. First, our early espionage targeted areas where rivals were not ahead of us, so it gained nothing; those resources would have been better saved. Second, our university investments and the open-border test produced no lasting benefit, although the test was inexpensive and taught us something important about Russia's limits. Third, we should have started building know-how one round earlier, which would have given our first advanced research attempts better odds. Fourth, because we were never sure whether a late round was the last, we stopped saving earlier than necessary; with more certainty about the game's length, we could have entered the final round with a larger budget. Luck also mattered, since our early advanced research failed more often than expected.

The broader lesson matches the course's conclusion that technological leadership depends on both people and institutions, and that leads can be gained and lost. Russia succeeded by using the pillar it had, accumulated knowledge, and by borrowing from the public investments of others, but it could not quickly build the talent and institutions that sustain leadership over the long run.

## 5. Comments on the game mechanics

The game captured important real-world trade-offs well, especially between openness and security and between funding public research and free-riding on others. We suggest three changes. First, investing in universities should more clearly increase a country's ability to attract talent; otherwise reversing brain drain is out of reach for poorer countries. Second, being caught spying carried no cost beyond public exposure; a modest diplomatic or economic penalty would make espionage decisions more realistic. Third, the score rewards breakthroughs but not how widely a country uses its technology. Ding argues that the Soviet Union produced impressive frontier achievements while failing to spread technology through its economy, and our own strategy of concentrating resources on a few high-value breakthroughs resembled that pattern. Rewarding adoption as well as invention would let the game test whether a country can turn discoveries into lasting national strength.
