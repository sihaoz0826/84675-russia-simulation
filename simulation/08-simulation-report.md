## 1. How gameplay unfolded

Russia began with the smallest budget in the game (wealth 4, or 8 dice a round), the most accumulated know-how (tacit knowledge 7, against 0 for the United States and China), a talent pool that shrank by 40% every round, and closed borders. Our brief proposed "save, then strike": grow the budget first, let rivals pay for public basic research, and then convert wealth into high-value research. The game unfolded in three stages.

| Round | Budget | Main moves | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 8 | 2 low-risk dice, 2 basic research, 1 university upgrade; saved 3 | +4 points; university level did not rise |
| 2 | 12 | Opened borders as a test; 2 low-risk dice; spies on the US and China; saved 6 | +2 points; rivals' basic research unlocked medium research for us |
| 3 | 20 | First medium dice; university upgrade; saved 10 | Low-risk research reached its 10-point cap; high-risk research unlocked |
| 4 | 32 | Closed borders; 15 basic research; medium and high-risk dice; saved 11 | All research dice failed; +2 medium points stolen from France |
| 5 | 46 | 40 basic research; know-how 29 to 51 | Both medium dice paid 6 (+12) |
| 6 | 46 | 41 basic research | High-risk jackpot: +36 points, counted three times |
| 7 | 46 | 40 basic research; spies on the US | +12 medium (medium cap reached); +3 low-risk points stolen from the US |

**Stage 1, building (Rounds 1-3).** We saved most of our budget, so our dice grew from 8 to 46 a round. We bought only the cheapest research dice and almost no basic research. Instead, we let rivals' spending, which counts 75% toward ours, unlock medium research by Round 3 and high-risk research by Round 4. By Round 3 our own basic research was only 3, yet our effective total was 30.8. We also tested whether Russia could attract talent by buying university upgrades and opening its borders in Round 2.

**Stage 2, frustration (Round 4).** We closed our borders, spent 15 dice on basic research to raise know-how before rolling, and bought our first full set of medium and high-risk dice. Every research die failed, and our first four medium dice in the game had all missed. Our only gain was 2 medium points copied from France.

**Stage 3, conversion (Rounds 5-7).** With 46 dice a round and research purchases capped at three dice, we put roughly 40 dice a round into basic research. Know-how rose from 29 to 83, past the levels at which medium research pays its 6-point reward 90% of the time and high-risk research pays its 36-point jackpot 70% of the time. Both medium dice hit in Round 5, our high-risk die hit the jackpot in Round 6, and both medium dice hit again in Round 7. We finished with 13 low-risk, 26 medium, and 36 high-risk points and know-how 83, for a score of about 336 by the game's formula: (13 + 2 x 26 + 3 x 36) x 1.89 + 2 x sqrt(23).

## 2. Our beliefs about Russia's progress

Because each team's points and know-how were hidden, we judged our position from public signals (wealth, talent, borders, and basic research) and from what our spies revealed. Early on we believed Russia was behind in points but ahead in strategy: US talent was exploding (6 to 117 by Round 6), but our spies showed that the US had only 2 low-risk points in Round 2 and China only 6 in Round 3, so the leaders were not running away with the game. At the same time, the US and China were also saving, and China's wealth passed ours by Round 2, which told us our budget advantage would not last.

Round 4 was our low point. China and France each showed 8 medium points while we had none of our own, and our dice had failed repeatedly. We debated whether our plan was wrong or merely unlucky; we concluded it was luck, since each medium die succeeds only half the time, and kept investing in know-how. After the Round 6 jackpot, we believed Russia had moved into first place, because a single jackpot adds about 200 points to a score. By the end we believed we were most likely first, with the main risk being that the United States or China had also hit a high-risk jackpot, which we could not observe. This uncertainty mirrored a theme from the course: it is hard to know what it means for a country to be "ahead" when rivals' most valuable technology is secret.

## 3. How and why our Round 1 strategy evolved

Our brief recommended staying closed all game, skipping education because Russia's talent drains away, and free-riding on rivals' basic research. Three things changed that plan.

**The professor's challenge: could Russia reverse its brain drain?** Before the game, Professor Canfil asked whether Russia might instead open its borders and invest in universities to attract talent, especially if other countries closed their doors over espionage fears. This echoed Zweig's account of how China worked to reverse its brain drain and Reif's warning that the United States could lose talent. We tested the idea cheaply: one university upgrade in Round 1 and opening our borders in Round 2, while we had few points that could be stolen. The test failed. Our university level did not rise, and our talent flow only improved from about 0.60 to 0.68, still below the level needed to grow talent. Even when the US closed its borders in Round 3, which in theory releases talent to others, Russia was not attractive enough to capture it. We concluded that in the game, as in reality, talent follows strong institutions, and Russia's weak institutions (131st in WIPO's Global Innovation Index) made the strategy unrealistic in the time available. We closed our borders again in Round 4 to protect our growing points.

**A shorter game than planned.** We had planned for 6-9 rounds; in class we learned the game would last only about 5-7. We cut our saving earlier and began buying basic research in Round 1 so that advanced research would open in time. When we did not know whether two or three rounds remained, we chose the option that worked in both cases: spending on know-how immediately rather than saving.

**Know-how became our main lever.** Once low-risk research was capped and research purchases were limited to three dice a round, the only productive use of our growing budget was basic research, which raises know-how before each round's dice are rolled. This fit Russia's real advantage. As Gilli and Gilli argue, the hands-on, tacit knowledge needed for complex technology cannot simply be copied or stolen, and in the game know-how could not be taken by spies. Our espionage also evolved: we spied at the low-risk level early, moved to medium and high-risk targets as rivals advanced, and stopped targeting countries once they closed. Throughout the game, decisions were made under the rule in our brief: a two-of-three majority, with the executive breaking ties.

## 4. Outcome and what we would change

By our calculation Russia finished with about 336 points. [Update with the official ranking once the instructor announces final scores.] We believe Russia performed well for three reasons. First, saving early turned the smallest budget in the game into one of the largest, which paid for the know-how that made our research rolls pay off. Second, free-riding on public basic research saved us dozens of dice: rivals' spending unlocked both advanced levels for us. Third, closing our borders protected our high-risk points, which counted three times and would have been the most valuable target in the game. Rivals also made costly choices. Spies were caught repeatedly (France twice in the final round, China and the US against each other), and the US and China opened and closed their borders several times, losing either talent or security each time. France never built the talent or wealth to reach the high-risk level.

In hindsight, we would change four things. First, our low-risk spies in Rounds 2 and 3 gained nothing, because rivals' low-risk points were never above ours; those dice would have been better saved. Second, our two university upgrades and the open-border test produced no lasting benefit, although the test was cheap and taught us something. Third, we should have started building know-how one round earlier: in Round 3, our know-how of about 14 gave a medium success only about a 46% chance of the 6-point reward, compared with 90% from Round 4 on, when we finally bought basic research in bulk. Fourth, because we treated Round 6 as possibly the last round, we saved nothing; had we known Round 7 would follow, saving in Round 6 would have given us a larger budget for Round 7. Luck also mattered: our first four medium dice all failed, when on average two of them should have succeeded, which likely cost us about 10 medium points before they were doubled and multiplied.

## 5. Comments on the game mechanics

The game captured real trade-offs well, especially between openness and security and between paying for public research and free-riding on it. We suggest three changes. First, university investment should visibly raise a country's attractiveness to talent; otherwise the strategy of reversing brain drain is out of reach for poorer countries. Second, the dice preview on the screen often differed from the real results, which confused teams; showing only the logged results would help. Third, being caught spying had no cost beyond a public message; a modest diplomatic or economic penalty would make espionage decisions more realistic.
