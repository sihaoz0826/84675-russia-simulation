# Russia: Simulation Report

Team 2: Steve (Executive and Technology Strategy Czar), Gloria (Intelligence and Immigration Czar), Tiger (Education Czar)

## 1. How the game unfolded

Russia started the game in a difficult position. It had the smallest budget of any country, its pool of skilled talent shrank every round, and its universities were weaker than its rivals'. Its main advantage was experience: Russia began with more accumulated know-how than any other country, reflecting its Soviet scientific legacy, and its closed borders meant no rival could steal its research. Our plan, set out in our strategy brief, was "save, then strike": grow our budget first, let other countries pay for public research, and then turn our wealth into high-value research later in the game. The game played out in three stages.

**Stage one: building (the early rounds).** We spent very little and saved most of our budget, because unspent money grew our economy and gave us a bigger budget in every later round. We bought only the cheapest and safest research. Instead of paying for basic research ourselves, we relied on the public research of the US and China, which also counted toward unlocking advanced research for us. We also tested a different idea, explained in Section 3: investing in universities and opening our borders to try to attract talent.

**Stage two: setbacks (the middle rounds).** By the middle of the game our safe research had reached its limit, and the advanced research levels had opened, largely thanks to our rivals' spending. We closed our borders again and began buying advanced research. This stage was frustrating. Our advanced research attempts failed several rounds in a row, and our only gains came from spying on rivals.

**Stage three: breakthrough (the final rounds).** With a much larger budget, we put most of our money into building know-how. The rules limited how much advanced research we could buy each round, and know-how made each attempt far more likely to pay off. This approach finally worked. Our medium-level research began paying its full reward, and in one round our radical research produced a major breakthrough, the single most valuable result in the game. In the last rounds we added more medium-level gains and copied some of the US's research through espionage.

## 2. Our beliefs about Russia's progress

Because every country's research results were secret, we had to judge our position from indirect clues: each country's wealth, talent, border policy, and public basic research, plus whatever our spies revealed. Early in the game we believed we were behind in research but ahead in strategy. The US was attracting talent much faster than anyone else, but our spies showed that the leading countries had not yet built much of a research lead. We also noticed that the US and China were saving money too, which told us our budget advantage would not last.

The middle of the game was our low point. China and France were showing advanced research results while ours kept failing, and China had become the wealthiest country. As a team we debated whether our strategy was wrong or whether we were simply unlucky. We decided that our odds were sound and that the failures were bad luck, so we kept investing in know-how instead of changing course.

After our radical breakthrough, our view changed. Because radical research counts for far more than any other result, we believed Russia had moved into the lead. Our remaining uncertainty was whether the US or China had also achieved a radical breakthrough, which we had no way to see. This experience reflected a theme from class: it is very hard to know which country is "ahead" in technology when the most important research is kept secret.

## 3. How and why our strategy evolved

Our Round 1 strategy, set out in the brief, was to keep our borders closed for the whole game, skip education because Russia's talent drains away, save heavily, and free-ride on other countries' basic research. Three developments changed it.

**The challenge to reverse brain drain.** Before the game, Professor Canfil asked whether Russia could do the opposite of our plan: open its borders and invest heavily in universities to attract talent, especially if other countries closed their doors out of fear of espionage. The idea echoed Zweig's account of how China worked to reverse its own brain drain and Reif's warning that the United States could lose talent. We tested it in the early rounds by investing in universities and opening our borders while we still had little research worth stealing. The test showed that Russia could not yet compete for talent. Our talent kept shrinking, and even when the US briefly closed its borders, Russia was not attractive enough to capture the talent it released. We concluded that talent follows wealth and strong institutions, and Russia's weak institutions, which are also its weakest point in real-world innovation rankings, made this path unrealistic in the time available. We closed our borders again to protect our growing research.

**A shorter game than expected.** During the simulation we learned the game would be shorter than we had planned for. Because the last round was never announced, and anything saved in the final round would be lost, we moved from saving to spending earlier than planned. When we were unsure how many rounds remained, we chose the option that would work either way: investing in know-how right away rather than saving for a round that might never come.

**Know-how became our main tool.** Since the rules capped how much research we could buy each round, our growing budget had only one productive use: building know-how, which made each research attempt more likely to succeed. This matched Russia's real strength. As Gilli and Gilli argue, the hands-on, tacit knowledge needed to produce advanced technology cannot simply be copied or stolen, and in the game, know-how could not be taken by spies. Our espionage also changed over time. We first spied on rivals' safe, low-level research, moved to their advanced research as they progressed, and stopped targeting countries once they closed their borders. Throughout the game we made decisions using the rule in our brief: a majority of the three of us decided, and the executive broke any ties.

## 4. Outcome and what we would do differently

Based on the information available to us at the end of the game, we believe Russia finished at or near the top. We think our strategy worked for three reasons. First, saving early turned the smallest budget in the game into one of the largest, which paid for the know-how that made our research succeed. Second, free-riding on other countries' public research saved us a great deal of money, because their spending unlocked advanced research for us. This is the public goods problem we discussed in class: basic research benefits everyone, so countries are tempted to let others pay for it. Third, keeping our borders closed protected our most valuable results from theft.

Our rivals also made costly choices. Spies were caught again and again, especially in the later rounds, and the US and China opened and closed their borders several times, giving up either talent or security each time they switched. France never built enough talent or wealth to reach the most valuable kinds of research.

Looking back, we would change four things. First, our early spying targeted areas where rivals were not ahead of us, so it gained nothing; those resources would have been better saved. Second, our university investments and the open-border test produced no lasting benefit, although the test was inexpensive and taught us something important about Russia's limits. Third, we should have started building know-how one round earlier, so that our first advanced research attempts would have had better odds. Fourth, because we were never sure whether a late round was the last one, we stopped saving earlier than turned out to be necessary; with more certainty about the game's length, we could have entered the final round with an even larger budget. Luck also played a role, since our early advanced research failed more often than expected and delayed our progress.

## 5. Comments on the game mechanics

The game captured important real-world trade-offs well, especially between openness and security and between paying for public research and free-riding on others. We would suggest three changes. First, investing in universities should more clearly make a country more attractive to talent; otherwise the strategy of reversing brain drain is out of reach for poorer countries. Second, the dice preview on the team screen often differed from the actual results, which confused teams, so showing only the real results would help. Third, getting caught spying had no cost beyond a public message; a small diplomatic or economic penalty would make espionage decisions more realistic.
